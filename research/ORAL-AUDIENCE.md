## Findings and implications to test

### OA-01 — Access includes agency

The Stringer Introduction and Ethical Exegesis sections argue that people with cultural and linguistic expertise should be able both to understand exegetical resources and to make interpretive choices. The distinction between control over others and capacity to act explains why supplying information alone may leave unequal decision-making intact. The Biblical Example section supplies the authors' theological rationale for accessibility; it is an attributed argument, not a new doctrine or app requirement established here.

Derived research question: when a group uses a resource, who can question it, propose an interpretation and influence the next action? Observe whether device control or facilitator practice suppresses those opportunities. Do not assume a larger media library resolves participation.

### OA-02 — Orality is contextual and multimodal

Stringer's Defining Orality section describes variable tendencies and movement between oral and textual practices. It identifies embodied experience, narrative reasoning and shared meaning-making, including repetition and communal memory. These are the extraction's explanatory categories, not a diagnostic test or fixed traits of every participant. Text-based institutions may fail to recognize knowledge created through other practices.

Derived research question: which combinations of discussion, repetition, movement, images, narration and text help particular people understand and express this passage? Investigate practices within the task rather than assigning a binary literacy label or assuming audio is sufficient.

### OA-03 — Physical access is a separate barrier

Stringer's Physical Access section describes infrastructure, device familiarity, affordability and language barriers. Reliance on outside expertise can persist when resources or support disappear. It reports efforts to improve offline availability and reduce language and licensing barriers; this is historical source reporting, not a current inventory or license verification.

Derived implication for offline preparation: test whether the intended language and resource remain usable on the actual device without connectivity, and who can obtain and operate them. A completed download does not establish comprehension or independent participation.

### OA-04 — Audio access and oral practice differ

Stringer's Accessible Formats section distinguishes audio rendering from processes that incorporate narrative, embodied activity and communal interpretation. It describes audio production/checking tools and internalization practices as examples; those examples do not place recording or translation checking inside this app's scope.

Derived implication: compare uninterrupted playback with opportunities to repeat, discuss and express meaning. A participant's preferred mode and the needs of the shared activity may differ; preserve alternatives rather than prescribing one universal sequence.

### OA-05 — Participation extends to creating knowledge

Building New Tables argues for recognizing oral theologians as contributors who create and share exegetical knowledge, beyond accommodating them as recipients. The Invitation returns to this unresolved challenge. This is a broader participation aim, not evidence that an app feature or a feedback form transfers interpretive authority.

Research question: how do participants' adaptations and disagreement reach content decisions, and what shows that the response affected a later iteration? Investigate the route and its authority before proposing submission or publishing features.

### OA-06 — Co-creation can change the resource process

The FIA Case Study reports a progression from an original five-step resource to a sixth articulation step after observing use and gathering feedback. It describes discussion, concrete media, wider narrative context and embodied internalization, with requests for more repetition, information and discussion. These are attributed historical accounts; they do not establish a current app method sequence or validate the journeys below.

The same section reports iterative testing of voices, images, maps and step organization, including a preference for human voices in certain focus groups despite favorable AI audio quality judgments. Do not generalize that preference to every community or use technical audio quality as a proxy for acceptance. Compare locally available choices with participants and record contrary preferences.

### OA-07 — Reported effects retain their study limits

Stringer's Impact section reports improved understanding, drafting and independent interpretive choices in several settings, including challenges to a source text's chosen expression. Those reports support investigating agency and understanding as outcomes rather than counting playback alone. The extraction's sample sizes, reach and outcome claims are not current app metrics, independently reproduced results or evidence of universal effect; no effectiveness estimate is derived here.

### OA-08 — Contextualization and production remain tensions

Stringer's Limitations section acknowledges variation across communities and the difficulty of contextualizing at scale. It also describes a gap between oral-facing resources and largely textual, individual production practices. Enabling end users to interpret is distinct from communities creating exegetical knowledge through their own processes.

Research implication: test local fit and participation in development separately. A successful session or localized recording cannot by itself establish that either broader problem is resolved.

### OA-09 — Technology does not explain people by itself

Huisman's Introduction questions research built on the prior assumption that students differ because of technology. It presents human action and technology as mutually involved and calls for reading arguments in context. Its observations concern writing education; they are not evidence about FIA participants.

The Conclusion likewise treats change as involving social, cultural, economic and psychological conditions alongside technology. Derived implication: describe participants' actual resources and practices before attributing behavior to phones, print or audio. A usability change after adding narration does not alone identify the cause.

### OA-10 — Avoid replacing one rigid category with another

Huisman's Conclusion uses the sensorium to discuss changing sensory emphasis and knowledge practices, while warning that a five-sense representation is culturally constructed and variation also exists within cultures. Speaking, recorded sound, reading print and interpreting images are not interchangeable activities. Description and mapping can help, but can also freeze a dynamic situation into misleading categories.

The passage retains a tension: it credits literacy and technology with opening possibilities, including Ong's broad developmental claims, while rejecting simple unilateral causation and a romanticized pristine oral past. It does not justify either dismissing literacy or imposing a universal progression. Its language about learning styles is not adopted here as a validated psychological classification.

Derived implication: use flexible descriptions of tasks, available knowledge and communication practices; revise them when observations disagree. Do not convert the sensorium into a mandatory five-channel interface checklist. Historical, political and classroom illustrations provide argumentative context, not new app requirements.

## Product research, not audience validation

The [role hypotheses](../product/PERSONAS.md#oral-audience-research-implications) and [journey research overlay](../product/JOURNEYS.md#oral-audience-research-overlay) translate these findings into questions. Physical access, format usability and participation require separate observations. Actual participant evidence, recruitment, local language fit, disability-specific design and agreed acceptance thresholds remain unfilled. Deaf and trauma-affected contexts are acknowledged in Stringer's scope discussion; this reading does not assess their specific needs.
